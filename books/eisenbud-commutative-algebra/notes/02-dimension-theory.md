# Dimension theory

Dimension theory converts geometric size into statements about prime ideals, generators, local rings, growth functions, transcendence degree, and fibers.

## 1. Krull dimension, height, and codimension

A chain of prime ideals

`p_0 ⊊ p_1 ⊊ ··· ⊊ p_r`

has length `r`. The Krull dimension of a ring is the supremum of lengths of prime chains.

For a prime `p`,

`ht(p) = dim A_p`

measures how many independent prime steps lie below `p`. Geometrically, in a well-behaved affine variety, height corresponds to codimension.

For `A/p`, its dimension measures the dimension of the irreducible closed subset `V(p)`. The two quantities `ht(p)` and `dim(A/p)` need not add to `dim A` in arbitrary rings; equality requires hypotheses. Notes should always state the hypotheses instead of importing geometric intuition silently.

## 2. Principal ideal theorem

Krull's principal ideal theorem says, in the Noetherian setting, that a prime minimal over one principal ideal has height at most one.

More generally, a prime minimal over an ideal generated by `r` elements has height at most `r`.

This gives the algebraic form of a geometric expectation:

> `r` equations can cut codimension by at most `r` at a minimal component.

Expected codimension is therefore a theorem only when additional regularity prevents dependencies among the equations.

### Systems of parameters

In a local ring `(A,m)` of dimension `d`, a system of parameters is a `d`-tuple `x_1,…,x_d` whose generated ideal is `m`-primary. Equivalently, the quotient has dimension zero.

Parameters encode the minimum number of local equations needed to collapse the germ to a zero-dimensional object, but they need not form a regular sequence. Cohen–Macaulay rings become special precisely because every system of parameters behaves homologically as well as dimension counting suggests.

## 3. Codimension one

Codimension one acts as the first serious meeting point of factorization, valuations, divisors, and normality.

For a Noetherian normal domain, localizations at height-one primes are discrete valuation rings. This converts codimension-one questions into valuation questions. Divisors can then record orders of vanishing along height-one subvarieties.

This material should cross-link directly to Fulton. Intersection theory repeatedly asks for cycles of codimension one, Cartier divisors, orders of vanishing, and the distinction between local principal behavior and global divisor classes.

## 4. Hilbert–Samuel functions and multiplicity

For a Noetherian local ring `(A,m)` and a finite module `M`, the function

`H_M(n) = length(M / m^{n+1}M)`

agrees for large `n` with a polynomial in `n`. Its degree is `dim M` under the standard hypotheses. The leading coefficient, after multiplying by `d!`, gives the Hilbert–Samuel multiplicity `e(M)` when `d = dim M`.

### Regular model

For

`A = k[x_1,…,x_d]_(x_1,…,x_d)`,

the quotient `A/m^{n+1}` has basis represented by monomials of total degree at most `n`. Hence

`length(A/m^{n+1}) = binomial(n+d, d)`.

The polynomial has leading term `n^d/d!`, so `e(A)=1`.

Multiplicity larger than one records local thickness/intersection behavior. This is one of the main bridges from local algebra into intersection theory.

## 5. Affine dimension and Noether normalization

For a finitely generated domain `A` over a field `k`, Noether normalization produces algebraically independent elements

`y_1,…,y_d ∈ A`

such that `A` is integral (equivalently finite) over the polynomial ring

`k[y_1,…,y_d]`.

The number `d` equals

`dim A = trdeg_k Frac(A)`.

This is a structural theorem, not merely a way to compute a number. It says every affine variety admits a finite map to affine space of its own dimension after a suitable choice of coordinates.

Consequences worth tracking:

- affine dimension becomes transcendence degree;
- chains of primes can be compared under integral extensions;
- generic projections become finite;
- induction on dimension gains a geometric map to affine space.

## 6. Elimination theory

Elimination asks which consequences of an ideal involve only a chosen subset of variables.

For

`I ⊂ k[x_1,…,x_n,y_1,…,y_m]`,

the elimination ideal

`I ∩ k[x_1,…,x_n]`

contains polynomial relations satisfied by the projected `x`-coordinates.

Geometrically, projection may fail to have closed image, so elimination naturally interacts with closure. Gröbner bases later make this constructive by choosing elimination orders.

## 7. Generic freeness

Generic freeness says, roughly, that finite algebra/module data over a domain becomes free after inverting one nonzero element of the base.

The geometric reading is essential: remove a proper closed subset of the base and a family that may behave irregularly globally acquires uniform module-theoretic behavior on a dense open set.

Generic freeness supplies a standard move:

1. identify bad behavior;
2. prove it disappears after localizing at one base element;
3. reason uniformly over the remaining dense open subset.

This move feeds directly into dimension-of-fibers arguments and later into generic smoothness-type reasoning when stronger hypotheses are available.

## 8. Dimension of fibers

For a finite-type morphism represented algebraically by `A → B`, a fiber over `p ∈ Spec A` has coordinate ring

`B ⊗_A κ(p)`.

The dimension of fibers measures how much freedom remains after fixing a base point.

The basic dimension heuristic

`dim(total) ≈ dim(base) + dim(generic fiber)`

becomes a theorem only under precise finite-type, irreducibility, catenary/equidimensional, or flatness hypotheses depending on the statement. Notes must keep those hypotheses visible.

Dimension jumps in special fibers signal failure of uniformity. The family `k[t,x]/(tx)` provides the simplest model: generic fibers are zero-dimensional, while the fiber at `t=0` has dimension one.

## 9. Dimension as intersection bookkeeping

Suppose `X` has dimension `n` and subvarieties `Y,Z ⊂ X` have codimensions `r,s`. A transverse or otherwise proper intersection suggests expected codimension `r+s` and dimension `n-r-s`.

Commutative algebra refines the slogan:

- principal ideal theorem bounds what equations can cut;
- regular sequences characterize situations where equations cut as expected homologically;
- Cohen–Macaulay hypotheses suppress certain hidden depth failures;
- multiplicities measure how intersections count when naive point counting fails.

That chain should be explicit in the Fulton bridge.

## Theorem ledger

- Krull principal ideal theorem.
- Height bound for an ideal generated by `r` elements.
- Existence and basic properties of systems of parameters.
- Height-one structure in normal Noetherian domains.
- Hilbert–Samuel polynomial and definition of multiplicity.
- Noether normalization.
- Equality `dim A = trdeg_k Frac(A)` for affine domains.
- Elimination theorem once Gröbner bases are available.
- Generic freeness.
- Dimension inequalities/equalities for fibers in exactly the hypotheses used by Eisenbud.
